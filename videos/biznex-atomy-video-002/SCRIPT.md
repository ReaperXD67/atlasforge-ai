Start with the picture. Put yourself in the center. The plan tracks activity through two
sides, usually described as a left leg and a right leg. Think of them as two lanes carrying
PV from qualifying product activity. The lanes do not have to grow evenly, but the official
general-commission table repeatedly looks at the smaller leg. That is why a giant number
on one side does not automatically produce the same commission score. Balance matters to
the calculation.

Now add Personal PV. On the current Atomy USA page, a person must first accumulate at
least ten thousand Personal PV before generating downline PV for the plan. The same page
says Personal PV may be added to the smaller leg when commission is calculated. Notice
what we are not doing: we're not inventing a dollar value for one PV. The official page
presents PV as the plan's tracking unit, so use the current company material for any
conversion or transaction-specific question.

Next come the five dealership classes. The current U.S. plan lists Sales Rep from ten
thousand through two hundred ninety-nine thousand nine hundred ninety-nine PV; Agent at
three hundred thousand or more; Special Agent at seven hundred thousand or more; Dealer
at one point five million or more; and Exclusive Distributor at two point four million or
more. The page also says dealership class can change monthly based on personal and team
sales. A rank name is therefore a qualification category, not a salary label.

Here is the part most presentations rush: the general commission. The official page says
forty-four percent of total sales PV is distributed among qualified members each week
according to the listed rate. Its table connects a dealership class and a daily smaller-leg
total to a score level. In plain English, the plan is not simply asking, 'How much did the
whole network do?' It is also asking, 'What qualification applies, and what happened on
the smaller side?'

Then there is mastership. The page says twenty percent of total sales PV is distributed
according to mastership level. The first listed level, Sales Master, requires Special Agent
status and at least two point five million Group PV under each leg. Higher masterships add
deeper structural conditions. Those requirements explain why a slide showing an incentive
is incomplete by itself. You also need the exact qualification path, the relevant period,
and the current rules underneath it.

A third official bucket is the education commission: six percent of a Center's total PV
may be paid to that Center for operating expenses. The plan also states that total
commissions to members do not exceed thirty-five percent of total sales income under the
applicable direct-selling rule. These percentages describe different allocations and bases.
Do not casually add them to a personal income forecast. A system-level pool is not the
same thing as an individual's likely payout.

So what does the Atomy plan do well? It gives you a visible structure to inspect: classes,
thresholds, score levels, smaller-leg logic, mastership conditions, and stated allocation
pools. That is much more useful than a slogan. But structure is not prediction. The page
does not tell a new viewer how many participants reach each level, what a typical participant
receives, or what typical net results look like after expenses. Those are separate evidence
questions.

That distinction is not negativity; it is basic decision hygiene. The Federal Trade
Commission says earnings representations must be truthful, substantiated, and representative
of what the intended audience is likely to achieve, including typical expenses. So when
someone explains this plan, listen for a clean boundary: official mechanics on one side,
evidence about typical outcomes on the other. If the two are blended together, ask them
to separate the claim from the chart.
